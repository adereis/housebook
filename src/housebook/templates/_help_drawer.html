Getting started

Housebook keeps your household's finances in one private place on your own computer. You don't type transactions in. An AI assistant reads your statements, receipts and tax forms and files them. This dashboard is where you look at the result and correct anything that's wrong.

How new data arrives

  • Import. You give the assistant files, such as a credit card statement, an Amazon order export or a pharmacy receipt. It works out what each one is and files it in your workspace.
  • Ingest. Housebook reads the filed documents into its database. Every new row starts out unverified.
  • Review. The assistant checks the new rows, fixing categories, spotting trips and pairing refunds. You confirm or correct the rest here.

Ask the assistant in plain words. For example, "import the statements in my Downloads folder" or "audit last month's spending".

Spending

The Spending page shows what you spent in a date range, by category and by month. Pick the dates at the top, and narrow the list by category or by card. The Review button shows only the transactions still waiting for a check.

  • Personal Total is your own spending. Work expenses you'll be reimbursed for are left out.
  • Needs Review counts transactions nobody has confirmed yet. Click it to list them.
  • Click any transaction to see its details: the statement it came from, and extra facts such as the original euro amount of a charge made abroad. Change its category there, and it's marked as confirmed by you.

What the totals leave out

Some rows are real statement lines but not real spending, so they stay in the database and out of every total:

  • Credit card payments. Paying off a card moves money you already counted when you made the purchases.
  • A purchase and its full refund, once they are linked. A $60 jacket you returned for $60 cancels out.
  • Amazon's bank-side duplicates. The itemized Amazon order export is counted instead of the single card line.
  • $0 rows, such as a gift-card redemption.

Partial refunds, cashback and other credits do stay. They lower your total, because they lowered what you really spent.

Trips

A trip gathers everything one journey cost, so you can see what a vacation came to. Trips appear as cards at the top of the Spending page.

  • Vacations & Trips open a trip page with the total, the cost per day, a day-by-day chart and every charge.
  • Work Events are trips you'll be reimbursed for. Clicking one filters the transaction list to it. Every charge on a work trip is marked Work (Reimbursable) and left out of your Personal Total.

How charges get onto a trip

The assistant suggests trips by spotting travel clusters: flights, hotels, and merchants far from home. You confirm them. A charge joins a trip because of where it happened, not only when. The groceries you bought at home during a vacation are not part of it. The flight you booked two months earlier is.

To move a single charge onto a trip yourself, open the transaction and choose the trip under Trip / Event.

Manual expenses

Manual expenses are costs that never show up on a statement. Examples are cash for a house cleaner, a check to the landscaper, or a premium paid from an account Housebook doesn't read.

Open the Manual Expenses tab on the Spending page to add one. Give it an amount, a category, and how often it repeats: monthly, yearly or once. A recurring expense counts in every period it covers until its end date, so a $200 monthly cleaner adds $2,400 to a year.

Projects

A project tracks what something you planned costs, measured against a budget. A bathroom renovation is the typical case.

Tell the assistant about it, for example "we're redoing the master bath with a $9,000 budget". It looks for matching charges such as hardware stores and plumbers, and asks you before adding any. Cash paid to a contractor goes in as a manual expense tied to the project.

Project charges still count in your Spending totals. A project groups spending, it doesn't hide it. For now, projects are managed through the assistant; the dashboard has no project page yet.

Income & Tax

The Income & Tax page collects each year's tax forms and adds them up. It is kept separate from spending. Examples of forms are a W-2 from your employer, 1099s from your bank, and a 1098 for mortgage interest.

  • The summary shows gross income, tax withheld, taxable income and your effective rate.
  • Deductions, retirement rollovers and HSA contributions are listed separately. So are foreign bank balances, if you must report them.
  • The tax estimate works out the year's federal tax from the forms on file, and whether you're likely to owe or get a refund. It's a planning aid, not a tax return.
  • The Tax Documents list shows every form and whether it has been checked.

HSA Shoebox

A health savings account (HSA) lets you pay medical bills out of pocket today and pay yourself back from the HSA tax-free, even years later. The catch is that you must be able to prove each expense if the IRS ever asks. The shoebox is that proof. It holds every medical expense, the documents behind it, and whether you've already paid yourself back for it.

How much proof each expense has

  • ✓+ Ironclad: three or more sources agree, usually the insurer's statement, the provider's receipt and the card charge.
  • ✓ Ready: two sources match one to one, such as a receipt and the card charge that paid it.
  • ? Weak: one payment covers several visits, so the visits must add up to the payment first. Two $65 therapy sessions paid with one $130 charge are an example.
  • [ ] Stub: not enough proof yet. Often it's a medical charge found on a card statement that is still waiting for its receipt.

Only Ready and Ironclad expenses count toward the Audit-Ready total, the amount you can safely withdraw.

Status

  • Unreimbursed: paid by you and not yet withdrawn from the HSA.
  • Pending: chosen for a withdrawal that hasn't happened yet.
  • Reimbursed: already withdrawn. It stays in the shoebox as a record.
  • Excluded: not HSA-eligible, such as teeth whitening. It is kept for the record but never counts. Show excluded reveals these rows.

Click an expense to open its documents, the card charge that paid it, and a history of every change made to it.

Review status

Every transaction records who has checked it. Rows still flagged for review are highlighted until someone confirms them.

  • Unverified: just imported. The category is a best guess.
  • Agent verified: the assistant has checked it.
  • User verified: you confirmed or corrected it. Your correction wins over any later guess.
  • Reconciled: a duplicate of a better source, such as the card line for an itemized Amazon order. It is hidden from totals.

Your data

Your ledger lives in a workspace folder on your computer, and Housebook itself never sends it anywhere. By default the dashboard only answers this computer. Reaching it from other devices at home requires setting up sign-in first.

The AI assistant is the exception. To import and review your documents it has to read them, so their contents reach the company that runs the assistant.

You can optionally sync the workspace to your own cloud drive, as a backup or to use more than one computer. The assistant only syncs when you ask it to.

To explore without your own data, run the demo with housebook-demo-seed. It shows five years of the fictitious Ledger family's finances.
